      Sheet Metal PC Case

      A computer case made from flat metal sheets that you can cut, bend and screw together yourself. The files are free to download, change and use for your own prototype.

      Unprototyped design. This design has not been physically validated yet. The CAD has been checked, but a real build still needs fabrication, component fit, electrical safety, grounding, cooling and load tests before it is ready for use.

      Fig. 1These are CAD images, not photographs of a finished case. The colours and finishes are illustrative.

      1What is it?

      It’s a box for your computer. It holds the parts together, makes room for cooling and gives everything a place to live on your desk.

      The custom chassis parts all start as flat sheet metal. They are cut, folded where needed and joined with ordinary fasteners. There is no welding and there are no custom moulded chassis parts.

      Switches, connectors and cables are bought separately. The gasket and protective pads are additional parts, so they are not included in the main sheet metal count.

      2Why I made it

      I wanted to make something I could give away. Most of what I design ends up on a client’s drive and nobody ever sees it again. This one is different. If you like it, download it, adapt it and try building it. It’s here to be used.

      3The look

      A plain metal box can feel a little anonymous. A few small details give this one its character without adding much to the construction.

      Fig. 2The feet, handle opening and lower step are all part of the same side plate.

      The sides. Each side plate works a bit like a sled. Its outline continues down into two feet and up above the body into a handle opening. That lifts the case off the desk without a separate set of legs. The handles are not load rated. Until they have been tested, use them for an empty case only.

      Fig. 3This detail uses studio backlighting on the side sheet. There are no built in lights.

      The vent. Each side has 546 round holes, 3.5 mm across, arranged on a staggered hexagonal grid. Solid bands trace the capsule shape around them. The pattern provides an opening for airflow, although its cooling performance still needs testing in a real build.

      The step line. A small step runs along the lower body. Without it, the case felt flat and unfinished. It is formed into the metal rather than added as a separate decorative strip.

      Fig. 4The surrounding shell and concealed carrier are hidden here so you can see the fascia. Purchased connectors are simplified in the CAD.

      The front. The power button, two USB-C ports, two USB-A ports and a 3.5 mm stereo audio output sit in one vertical panel. Everything is lined up to keep the front easy to read.

      Two USB-C ports need two independent supported Type-E motherboard hosts. If your board has only one, use the supplied fascia with a single USB-C port. The USB-A pair uses one supported 19 pin header. Audio comes from a rear stereo output. It does not connect to the motherboard HD Audio header or provide a headset microphone input.

      The panel cables and header adapters are passive parts, and the complete connection needs testing. The design is for standard 5 V host operation. It does not claim Thunderbolt, USB4, DisplayPort Alt Mode, USB Power Delivery or certification of the complete USB chain. The I/O instructions explain the selected hardware and the checks for plug orientation, hot plugging and current delivery.

      4How it’s made

      The full arrangement uses 19 main sheet metal pieces from 18 cut designs. That includes the optional side fan bracket and GPU support. Small shims, the gasket, fasteners and electronics are additional. Choose one fascia option rather than installing every alternative.

      Fig. 5The actual cut geometry is shown at one common scale. This is not a cutting nest. Alternative fascias, test coupons, shims and the gasket are not shown.

      1. 1. Cut the parts. Use the developed DXFs and have your shop check its process with the supplied test coupons. The sides are 1.5 mm 5052-H32 aluminium. The wrap, rear, tray and most brackets are 1 mm mild steel. The fascia is 1 mm aluminium, and the nutstrip and GPU support are 3 mm mild steel. Follow the material schedule for each part.
      2. 2. Make the bends. Follow the drawings and bend sequence. Check the bend allowance and tool access with your shop first. The wrap blank is about 1,238 mm long and includes formed offsets, so it will not suit every small press brake.
      3. 3. Clean the edges. Deburr before bending and check again afterwards. A file or abrasive paper will take care of sharp edges that could catch fingers or damage cables.
      4. 4. Check the assembly. Trial fit the parts with screws, nuts, rivet nuts and standoffs. Most chassis joints use M3 screws. The hardware list gives the fixings for the computer components. Fit only the motherboard standoffs your board actually needs.
      5. 5. Apply the finish. Once the fit is right, take the case apart for finishing and then reassemble it. You can brush the metal or have it powder coated. Bare steel needs corrosion protection. Keep the specified bonding faces and connector seats free of coating, and allow for the thickness of the finish.

      Fig. 6This view shows how the main parts relate to each other. It is not a disassembly sequence. Computer components and some fasteners are hidden for clarity.

      The build guide includes the checks to complete before cutting a full set of parts. One is a documented 0.4572 mm PCI setback discrepancy that needs checking with real brackets, cards and slot covers. The digital checks do not replace this test or the electrical, grounding, thermal and load tests.

      5What fits inside

      These are design targets, not a guarantee that every combination of parts will fit.

      • Motherboard: ATX up to 304.8 × 243.84 mm, microATX up to 243.84 × 243.84 mm, or Mini-ITX at 170 × 170 mm.
      • Graphics card: up to 340 mm long, 150 mm projection and 65 mm thick. There is a separate 35 mm allowance for the connector. Check the actual card and cable bend.
      • Power supply: standard ATX with a 150 × 86 mm cross section and a depth of 140 to 180 mm, plus a 60 mm cable allowance.
      • CPU cooler: a height target of 170 mm. Check it against your socket, RAM and motherboard layout.
      • Fans: one 140 mm fan at the bottom and one at the rear, with an optional 120 mm side fan on a removable bracket.
      • Storage: one 2.5 inch SSD that is 7 mm thick.

      The main case outline is 302 mm wide, 410 mm deep and 520 mm high. Including the modelled screw heads and rear rail, allow 306.8 × 422 × 520 mm. E-ATX boards and radiators are not claimed configurations.

      Measure your parts before you cut anything. Metal is a lot less forgiving than a 3D print.

      6What you get in the download

      The download includes 29 developed DXFs. These cover the main cut designs, three alternative fascias, six process test coupons and two designs for the shims and gasket.

      There are STEP files for the full assembly and manufactured sheet parts, editable FreeCAD files for the assembly and flat patterns, and Python source tools.

      The 41 page A3 guide covers the dimensions, bends, fasteners and assembly steps. You also get the sheet part and hardware lists, purchasing references, I/O instructions, digital verification records, license and notices.

      Download the prototype files, ZIP, 10.7 MB

      Read the build guide, PDF, 3.5 MB · Sheet parts list · Hardware list

      This is a free community design that has not been physically validated. It is not a manufactured product or a certified kit. Your shop needs to qualify the cutting and bending process, and the finished build needs testing.

      7Make it your own

      Change the vent pattern. Make it taller. Paint it Plum Wine. That is the point of sharing it.

      Changes to the material, thickness, size or hole pattern affect the bends, stiffness, clearances and cooling. A brass version would need its own engineering and tests, rather than just a different material at the cutting shop.

      If you build one, I would love to see it. Send me a picture and I might feature it here.

      8License

      The design is shared under the CERN Open Hardware Licence Version 2, Permissive, identified as CERN-OHL-P-2.0. You can build it, change it, share it and sell it, subject to the license and its notice requirements. Commercial use is allowed.       Automatic rubber-tapping machine

      A machine that goes around a rubber tree and makes the cut that lets the latex flow, so a person does not have to do it by hand. This is the simple story of how I designed it, how I tested it on a computer, and what I learned.

      StatusDesigned and tested on a computer. Not built yet.
      What I did3D design, strength tests and motion tests
      Client versionCase study on gimli.works

      Tested

      Made with
      FreeCAD and free simulation tools

      1What is it?

      Rubber comes from a white, milky sap called latex. To collect it, someone makes a thin cut in the tree’s bark, and the latex slowly drips out. That job is called tapping.

      My machine does that job. It clamps onto a tree, moves a small knife around the trunk, and makes the cut by itself. I have drawn the whole thing in 3D and tested it on a computer. I have not built it yet.

      Fig. 1The whole machine. Two rings hold on to the tree, the cutting head sits on a column, and the control box is on the side.

      2Why build it?

      Tapping by hand takes skill and a lot of hours. The cut has to be at the right angle and deep enough, but never all the way through the bark, and no tree trunk is ever a perfect shape.

      A machine has to handle all of that. It must hold on to the tree, travel around it, press a knife against the bark, and pull away safely. So I split the job into four small ones: hold on, travel, move in, and cut. That way I can work on each one on its own.

      3How does it work?

      Two rings clamp around the trunk. They can be adjusted, so the same machine fits trees of different sizes. A small carriage rides around the ring, and a screw moves the cutting part up and down. The electronics live in a separate box at the side, away from the sap and the rain.

      Figs 2 to 8

      1 / 7

      At the end of it all sits the cutting head, and it does two things. First it moves a short way towards the bark so the knife touches it. Then a motor rocks the knife back and forth just a few degrees, and that small rocking is what makes the cut. How deep the cut goes is a different question, and my tests do not answer it.

      Between the motor and the knife there are a few small parts, like a coupling and a shaft. I kept them as separate pieces on purpose, so I could study each one by itself.

      Fig. 9The cutting head up close: the motor, the part that moves it towards the bark, and the metal bracket that holds it all.

      4How did I test it?

      Before building anything, I tested the design on a computer. This is called simulation, and I did two kinds.

      Will it bend or break? The computer cuts each part into tens of thousands of tiny pieces and works out how hard each piece is pushed. This is called FEA. I used it on the three parts that take the cutting force: the knife, the shaft that turns it, and the bracket that holds the head.

      Does the knife do what the motor tells it? I made a simple model of the head and let the computer play it forward in time, a bit like a physics game. This is called a dynamics simulation.

      For the pushing force I used 100 newtons, about the weight of a 10 kilogram bag. That number is my own guess, not something I measured on a real tree.

      Fig. 10The three parts I tested, shown in their real positions: the bracket, the shaft and the knife.

      5What did the tests show?

      The knife and the shaft hardly moved at all. The bracket that holds the head bent the most. It moved about a third of a millimetre, which sounds tiny, but it is about a hundred times more than the knife and the shaft.

      Fig. 11How the bracket bends under the test force. Brighter gold means it moves more. I stretched the bend 30 times so you can see it.

      In the motion test, the knife follows the motor, but a little late and not quite far enough. I asked it to rock 3 degrees each way, and it reached about 2.7. The lag comes from the soft coupling and the small gaps between parts.

      VideoSixteen seconds of the computer model in motion. The head moves in, the knife rocks, and then it pulls back out.

      Fig. 12What I asked for against what really happens. The knife is always a little behind.

      Then I made the cutting resistance bigger, and that changed the answer more than anything else. The harder the tree pushed back, the worse the knife followed, and the harder the motor had to work.

      Fig. 13More resistance means bigger errors. Each bar is one test.

      6What changed in the design?

      The tests changed my plan in two ways.

      First, the weak spot is the bracket, not the knife. So the next version needs a much stiffer bracket, and that comes before anything else.

      Second, how hard the tree pushes back matters more than the fine details of my model. The most useful thing I can do next is measure that on a real tree, because one real measurement will teach me more than more simulation.

      7What I don’t know yet

      • I have not built it. Everything here is a design and a set of computer tests.
      • The forces are guesses. I need to measure the real ones.
      • The stress answers are not settled. When I made the computer pieces smaller, the bending answers stopped changing, but the stress answers kept moving, so I do not trust them yet.
      • It cannot pull itself back if the power fails. That means I cannot say it is safe for the tree.
      • The small part that pushes the head in may be asked to do too much. It is only built to work about a fifth of the time, and my plan asks for more than a third.
      • The two tests do not talk to each other yet. One tells me about bending and the other about motion, but they are separate.

      Next, I want to measure real parts, test the head on a bench by itself, and check how the force really travels into the bracket. I have not looked at heat or airflow inside the control box at all, so that could be a simulation for another day.

      Some of the CAD, the analysis and the pictures were made with help from AI tools. These are early results, not proof that the machine works.
