      About

      Mechanical engineer designing robotic and mechatronic machines, from first idea to tested prototype.

      I love understanding how things move, and then making them move better. Most ideas start in my notebook as rough sketches, then go into CAD, simulation and the 3D printer, and round the loop again until the design holds up in the real world. Every machine teaches me something the last one didn’t, and that is what keeps me curious.

      This site brings all of it under one roof: the machines I design, what each one taught me, and my thoughts along the way. Some of it is finished work. A lot of it is still in progress, and I share it as it happens.

      Sheet metal PC case

      I designed a PC case around a simple idea: let the sheet metal do more of the work. The sides become feet, handles and vents; a single dark wrap ties the body together.

      01One panel, several jobs

      I wanted the case to have a recognisable silhouette without relying on extra trim. The almond sides extend below the body to form feet and above it to form the handle openings. The charcoal wrap brings the front, roof and floor together.

      The lower step is formed into the metal, not attached afterwards. It changes how the light catches the side and makes the transition to the feet feel deliberate. The vent follows the same idea: repeated round holes, interrupted by solid capsule bands.

      A closer look at the sides
      • Each side has 546 round holes, 3.5 mm across. The pattern is a design for ventilation, not evidence of measured cooling performance.
      • The warm glow in the close view comes from external studio illumination. There are no installed lights in the model.
      • The integrated handles are not load rated. Use them only for an empty case until physically load tested.

      02Make room for more than the parts

      A motherboard outline is only the beginning. Cables need room to turn, plugs need room to enter, and fasteners still need to be reached. I worked around those spaces as well as the component bodies.

      The interior view shows that layout rather than a particular branded computer. Keeping the reference parts simple makes the important relationships easier to see: board to rear panel, graphics card to front, power supply to cable space.

      Component targets and space reservations
      • The layout targets ATX, microATX and Mini ITX boards. Confirm the chosen board’s mounting holes, connector positions and rear interfaces; do not install unused standoffs.
      • Graphics card envelope: 340 × 150 × 65 mm, plus a separate 35 mm connector reserve. Cooler height target: 170 mm. Actual cable bends and neighbouring components still need checking.
      • ATX power supply depth: 140 to 180 mm, with 60 mm reserved for cables. Bottom and rear fan targets are 140 mm; the optional side fan is 120 mm. These are not guaranteed component combinations.
      • Main outline: 302 × 410 × 520 mm. Allow 306.8 × 422 × 520 mm including screw heads and the rear rail. E-ATX boards and radiators are not claimed configurations.
      • The interior picture hides one side and the optional side fan and its bridge. The other computer shapes are dimensional references, not detailed manufacturer models.

      03A simple strip with a lot behind it

      Power, four USB ports and stereo audio sit in one vertical panel. The visible arrangement is simple. Behind it, each connector has its own mounting, clearance and cable requirements.

      The USB modules share a concealed carrier rather than relying on the thin face panel alone. The design also accounts for plug access, screw engagement and cable restraint. Those small details are what turn a row of openings into a connection layout.

      Connections and their limits
      • Each USB C socket uses a separate Newnex U4S5C01C08-010 cable and Delock 67234 adapter to an independent supported Type E host. If only one host is available, develop the documented single C configuration rather than using a splitter.
      • The two USB A sockets use Newnex USR2-AA-1M leads and one Delock 82941 adapter to a two port 19 pin header.
      • The FRONTX P0902_036 audio lead extends a rear stereo output. It is not a microphone input, a motherboard audio header connection or an amplifier.
      • Use standard 5 V host operation. Complete chain speed, certification, PD, alternate display functions, USB4 and Thunderbolt capability are not claimed. Test enumeration, current, plug orientations and hot plugging on the complete connected chain.
      • The image isolates the fascia and simplified connector allocations. The carrier, surrounding shell and some fixings are hidden. Read the I/O notes for actual mounting stacks, finish allowances, plug limits and cable support.

      04Design for the bend and the screwdriver

      The model contains 19 main sheet pieces from 18 designs, including three optional support pieces. Most of the shape comes from the folds and the way those pieces meet, rather than from additional decorative parts.

      I worked through the joints as part of the design: screw lengths, washer clearance, the rear card rail and access for assembly. A clean outside is only useful if the inside can be put together and serviced.

      Manufacturing reference, not cutting instructions
      • The sides are 1.5 mm aluminium and the fascia is 1 mm aluminium. The wrap, rear and tray use 1 mm steel; the nutstrip and optional graphics card support use 3 mm steel. The sheet BOM identifies the smaller parts.
      • The provisional wrap development is about 1,238 mm long. A fabricator must confirm brake capacity, segmented tool access, material, bend allowances, springback and finish before making a full set.
      • The release contains formed STEP geometry and reference drawings. Flat cutting files and automatic unfolding are not supplied. A fabricator must develop and qualify their own cutting patterns; do not trace the illustration or scale PDF artwork for cutting.
      • The guide includes reference alternatives and test coupon dimensions. These are not additional STEP parts. Choose the required configuration rather than counting every alternative as another installed piece.
      • Deburr handles, vents and cable passages. Protect bare steel from corrosion, allow for coating thickness and keep specified bonding faces and connector seats clear.

      05See how the shell is organised

      The main panel groups separate and return in this short design animation. It is a quick way to see how the side plates, wrap, tray and rear frame relate.

      The motion is illustrative, not a validated assembly path or a physics simulation. Computer reference parts, optional supports and most loose hardware are omitted so the main structure stays readable.

      06Evidence, not just an image

      The result is more than an exterior model. It includes the formed parts, selected connection hardware, assembly notes, purchasing references and a drawing set. The exported geometry has been reopened and checked; the documentation keeps the remaining physical questions explicit.

      • Two valid STEP exports: the reference inclusive assembly and the 19 formed sheet instances.
      • Coordinated mounting and connection details, including a concealed USB carrier and documented cable space.
      • A 41 page A3 guide covering reference dimensions, bends, interfaces and the qualification plan.

      One small rear interface discrepancy is deliberately left visible in the notes. More decimal places would not settle it. A real bracket, card and slot cover in a physical gauge will.

      What the digital checks do and do not establish
      • Fresh STEP imports contain 290 valid solids in the assembly and 19 in the sheet file. The assembly includes hardware and component reference envelopes, so its total volume is not enclosure material volume or mass.
      • Earlier assembly, clearance and cable routing records are retained with their original scope. They are not new physical tests, and they do not establish universal hardware compatibility.
      • The documented PCI bracket setback difference is 0.4572 mm. Qualify the rear interfaces with a physical gauge, real brackets, cards and slot covers before final metalwork.
      • The first prototype must establish forming accuracy, finished component and connector fit, electrical operation, bonding continuity, temperatures under load and structural capacity. Powder coating is insulating, so measure continuity on the finished assembly.
      • Preserve the power supply’s protective earth. Never open, drill or modify the supply or improvise mains wiring. Get qualified electrical help where needed. The handles remain unrated and limited to empty case handling until tested.

      07Explore the design

      I am sharing the formed geometry and the reference material so you can inspect the layout, understand the decisions and adapt the design. This is a planning reference release, not a complete fabrication kit.

      Download the engineering reference package (ZIP, 5.8 MB)

      • Reference drawings and build guide (PDF)

      The two STEP files contain solid geometry in assembled or formed positions. They do not carry the original feature tree, sketch constraints or an automatic link to flat patterns. Cutting files are not included; manufacturing development and shop qualification remain part of the next stage.

      08The next useful step

      The most useful decisions in this project were often the small ones: where a screw can be reached, what sits behind a port, and how much room a cable needs after the connector has fitted.

      The next step is a first physical prototype, beginning with forming tests and the rear interface gauge. That is where the digital assumptions meet a workshop’s tools and real hardware.

      Most of what I design ends up on a client’s drive. I wanted this project to be something people could explore and learn from. If you adapt it, tell me what you changed and what you found.

      Four wing flapping robot

      I designed a four wing mechanism and traced its loads through the transmission to a proposed test bench. One fixed spring gave almost all the calculated benefit of three selectable settings.

      01Start with the interfaces

      A wing is only one part of a flapping machine. The bearings, shafts, spring anchors, steering links and wiring all need somewhere to go, without blocking one another.

      I developed a compact reference with four 140 mm wings, two independent drives and removable torsion bars. The planning mass is about 42 g. Each drive uses a 20:1 reduction and a four bar linkage. The steering mechanism has a geometric design range of ±17°.

      I worked through the small interfaces as well as the overall layout: root bearings, retained shafts, clamp seats, motor mounts, cable paths and access for assembly. Those details make the concept inspectable, even where qualification is still needed.

      Reference and interface details

      The displayed aircraft is the DC1/WM1 reference. Its planning mass is 41.95 g, including allocated provisions; it is not a weighed prototype. The approximately 330 mm design span is a planning envelope, not a claim that every neutral pose has that projected tip distance. The reference contains 495 geometric leaves and 540 solids. The later 42.24 g correction candidate was not adopted.

      Interface work includes upper and lower drive packaging, root bearing seats, shaft retention, spring clamps and anchors, steering links and servo allocation, battery and board provisions, wiring clearance and fastener access. Finite prescribed rigid pose checks do not prove flexible wing or spring clearance in operation. Strength, joint retention and fatigue qualification remain unresolved.

      02Give the reversing wings somewhere to store energy

      At the end of each stroke, a wing slows and reverses. A spring can store some of that energy and return it during the next part of the motion.

      I integrated four removable formed torsion bars into the drive structure. The reference uses 0.7 mm wire with a 45 mm active length. Removable bars make stiffness a testable design choice rather than a permanent commitment.

      The motion below uses the actual linkage geometry. It is a prescribed sequence, not a prediction of how the motors will respond.

      03Make the comparison fair

      I compared no spring, one fixed setting and three selectable settings across the same prescribed mission: 17, 18.5 and 20 Hz with equal weighting.

      The first selectable menu was worse than the best fixed setting. I then optimised both choices against the same objective. That distinction mattered more than the attractive idea of changing stiffness.

      The study covered 1,694 stiffness and frequency combinations. I also checked numerical integration and examined 1,000 conditional scenarios to see how dependent the conclusion was on the assumptions.

      04One spring gives almost all the benefit

      The best fixed spring reduced calculated mechanical input power from about 2.14 W to 1.80 W, roughly 16% compared with no spring.

      Three selectable settings improved that by only 0.2% compared with the best fixed spring, or about 3.6 mW. That is a small margin to spend on selection hardware, mass, friction and service complexity.

      At 20 Hz, the peak ideal input shaft equivalent was about 28% lower with the fixed spring and 32% lower with the selected setting, compared with no spring. These are mechanical model results, not battery savings.

      Detailed values, baselines and assumptions

      Values here are rounded to four significant figures. The full precision results are in the repository data.

      The objective is mean positive mechanical input power for both drives, equally weighted across 17, 18.5 and 20 Hz. No spring: 2.144 W. Best fixed grid setting: 1.802 W at stiffness multiplier 1.625. Optimised menu: 1.798 W, with multipliers 1.375, 1.625 and 1.900. Its advantage over the fixed grid setting is 0.1991%, or 3.588 mW.

      The separate refined fixed search used multiplier 1.63125 and found a menu advantage of 0.1991%, or 3.587 mW. The original menu was 10.85 mW worse than the best fixed grid setting. Nine of the 1,000 conditional scenarios gave a negative menu advantage. These are hypothetical conditional cases, not a measured population or confidence interval.

      The settings are abstract spring torque scalings, not qualified parts or an implemented switching mechanism. The map retains all 1,694 sampled cases, including 137 with negative savings, and the original −60 to +60% colour scale. Pair stiffness is twice the single bar secant stiffness times the multiplier. The circle at 20 Hz and 136.0 N·mm/rad marks a sampled reference with 16.13% saving versus no spring at that frequency, not a feasible design optimum. The dashed line is a simplified storage frequency estimate, not an assembled modal result.

      At 20 Hz, absolute cycle peak ideal input shaft equivalents per drive are 2.325, 1.678 and 1.571 mN·m for no spring, fixed and selected settings. These are output torques divided by the ideal 20:1 ratio, not measured motor torque.

      05Keep the decision, not just the favourable number

      My takeaway is to start with a fixed spring and measure it properly before making the transmission more complicated.

      The result depends on how negative mechanical work is treated. With ideal recovery, a conservative spring does not reduce signed cycle work. The stiffness map also includes combinations that make the positive power objective worse. I have kept those outcomes in the downloadable evidence.

      Model limits and unresolved qualification

      Motion is imposed and aerodynamic loads are unvalidated. The work does not establish electrical efficiency, equal lift performance, endurance, hover, full cycle converged airflow, or strength and life qualification. The simple reference storage estimate is 23.78 Hz dry and 14.33 Hz with the legacy added inertia hypothesis. The selected multiplier 1.9 at 20 Hz also misses the earlier 16 to 18 Hz storage target. Material properties, retention, fatigue and assembled frequency need measurement.

      06Design a way to measure it

      I designed a dedicated bench around a reduced test article, a located fixture and a proposed six axis force sensor. Opposed contacts, liners and keepers locate the article; shims and accessible fasteners support adjustment and inspection.

      In the normal measurement arrangement, loads pass through the sensor. A separate severe proof arrangement removes the sensor and its adapters, replacing them with a dedicated bypass. It is not a parallel brace that quietly short circuits the measurement.

      I also laid out the enclosure, access door, latch and interlock mounts, cable routes and service access. The inspection view hides selected panels so the load path is visible. It is not an operating configuration, and the enclosure has no containment rating.

      Download the models and numerical evidence

      Before an energised test

      All ten physical qualification holds remain open. The bench model has 1,096 solids, including the complete enclosure. The proposed sensor and its overload path are not qualified by the rendering. Severe proof through the measurement sensor is prohibited. Neither the exchange models nor the inspection images authorise manufacture, energisation or an unguarded test.

      07Turn the uncertainty into measurements

      The next step is gated bench qualification: characterise the bars, verify retention and fixture stiffness, establish sensor and drive limits, and then measure torque and energy under controlled motion.

      I would use those measurements to decide whether the spring benefit survives real losses and whether selectable stiffness is worth revisiting. The purpose of the model is to make that experiment sharper, not to stand in for it.
